## Overview

Capture every rally, smash, and epic point with this specially designed **3D printed phone holder** for padel courts!
Built with **two powerful suction cups**, it locks firmly onto the glass walls, giving you a rock-steady angle for recording your matches.

Padel courts are enclosed by glass and mesh walls specifically so the ball can bounce off them during play, and those same glass panels turn out to be an ideal spot to mount a phone for filming a match from a wide, elevated angle. The problem is that most phone mounts are designed for a car dashboard or a tripod, not a vertical sheet of glass that a ball is going to slam into repeatedly during the match. A single suction cup can hold a phone's weight just fine sitting still, but it is not enough to resist the vibration and shock of the ball hitting the glass a few centimeters away, which is exactly what tends to knock single-cup mounts loose mid-match.

✨ **Why you’ll love it:**

- **Super stable shots** – dual suction keeps your phone safe and steady.
- **Universal design** – fits most smartphones, no extra tools needed.
- **Quick & easy setup** – mount it in seconds, remove it just as fast.
- **Lightweight & portable** – toss it in your sports bag and you’re ready to go.

Whether you’re looking to **improve your game, share highlights**, or just keep memories of your best rallies, this holder makes recording effortless.

The picture shows several test versions I created before settling on the final design, which is printed on the right in **PLA** (red) and **ABS** (black). My first attempt used a spring mechanism to keep the jaws closed, but it proved to be quite challenging. I eventually opted for a simpler and more reliable solution: interlocking saw teeth.

A spring-loaded clamp sounds like the obvious solution on paper, but designing one that is both strong enough to hold two suction cups under load and easy to print without extra hardware turned out to be a lot more finicky than expected, between finding the right spring geometry, tuning the wall thickness so it would not snap, and getting the initial tension right so it clamped hard enough without over-stressing the print. The interlocking saw-tooth ratchet I ended up with instead skips all of that: you press the two halves together, the teeth catch and hold, and there is no spring geometry to get wrong or wear out over time. As a design lesson, it was a good reminder that the mechanically simplest solution is often the one that survives repeated use best, even if it is less elegant on paper than a spring mechanism.
